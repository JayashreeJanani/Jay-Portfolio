An Idea That Surprised Me

What if jealousy is information?

I liked the way the book reframed jealousy. Instead of immediately treating it as something shameful, it can sometimes point toward a blocked desire — something another person has that makes us realise we may want something similar for ourselves.

The useful question then becomes:

“What is this feeling trying to show me about what I want?”

That turns jealousy from something used against ourselves into something that can help with direction and self-awareness.

My Biggest Takeaway

Be someone you can count on.

We spend so much time hoping someone else will encourage us, validate us, or tell us we're doing well. What stayed with me from this book was the idea that we can learn to become that person for ourselves too.

My Full Review

What I Wrote After Finishing It

My original notebook reflections, rewritten for the screen.

The High 5 Habit by Mel Robbins is centred around something that sounds incredibly simple: giving yourself a high five in the mirror. But underneath that small action is a much bigger idea about learning to support yourself.

One of the things that stood out most to me was how much validation we tend to seek from other people. We want someone else to tell us that we are doing well, that we are capable, or that we should be proud of ourselves. The book asks us to begin building some of that validation internally.

I especially liked the idea that confidence in yourself is something you continue developing throughout your life. It isn't necessarily a personality trait that some people simply have and others don't.

Another message that stayed with me was the importance of being aware of our energy. The book encourages us to notice what gives us energy and what continually depletes it. That doesn't mean life should always be easy, but it does mean we should pay attention to what certain environments, relationships, and situations are doing to us.

I also highlighted the reminder that when we are tired, we don't necessarily need to quit. Sometimes we simply need to rest. I found that distinction important because exhaustion can make something meaningful feel impossible even when what we really need is recovery.

The discussion around other people's opinions also stood out. Someone can have an opinion about you without that opinion becoming a definition of who you are. Learning not to absorb every judgement personally is part of protecting your own confidence.

One of the more unexpected ideas for me was the section on jealousy. Instead of simply treating jealousy as a bad emotion, the book describes it as something that can sometimes point toward a blocked desire.

If I feel jealous because someone has achieved something, the useful question may not be, “Why am I like this?” Instead I can ask, “Is this showing me something I want for myself?”

I liked that approach because it turns jealousy into information. Rather than using the feeling as a weapon against ourselves, it can sometimes help us understand our own ambitions and direction.

Overall, the book made me think about what it means to genuinely be on my own side. Supporting yourself doesn't mean believing you're perfect. It means becoming someone who can encourage yourself, recognise your progress, rest when needed, and continue moving forward.

If there is one idea I would carry from the book, it is this: the person in the mirror deserves your encouragement too.
